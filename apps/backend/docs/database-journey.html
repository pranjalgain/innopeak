Database Journey

Twelve things a person or a background job actually does in InnoPeak, in the order they happen — and next to each one, the exact tables, columns, and enum values that move. Same schema as apps/backend/src/db/drizzle/migrations/0000_init.sql, read as a story instead of a table list.

Before the journey five clusters, one thread

Where the 19 tables live

Every domain table carries a tenant_id — that's the thread running under all five clusters below. Sync & Audit isn't a step in the journey; it's a trigger (log_db_changes()) sitting behind every write in the other four, which is why it's drawn underneath rather than in line with the rest.

Stage 01 Login & OnboardingSignup screens

Someone signs up

A business name and one click on "Continue with Microsoft" — that's the whole form, because Entra ID hands back a verified email InnoPeak doesn't need to double-check.

  • The password path (not exposed in this build's UI, but still live at the API) would leave status = pending_verification and add a password_reset_tokens row with purpose = verify_email instead of writing straight to user_identities.
  • If this tenant should only accept sign-ins from their own Microsoft directory, a tenant_identity_providers row (provider = entra_id) gets written here too — that's the one open question still waiting on the client.

Stage 02 OnboardingConnect — connect & confirm

They connect the business

Two different Googles in one screen: Google Business Profile for review data, nothing to do with the Microsoft account that just signed them in.

  • review_provider_connections.provider and user_identities.provider are unrelated columns on unrelated tables — the login method never constrains the review-data source.

Stage 03 OnboardingConnect — importing history

187 old reviews come in

The one-time backfill: every review Google has ever recorded for this location, plus whatever reply already went out, imported as historical rows rather than live ones.

  • idx_review_responses_review_id_imported is a unique index on (review_id) WHERE source = 'imported' — if the backfill job crashes and retries, it can't double-import the same reply.

Stage 04 background — every 15 minutes

The poller checks for new reviews

No screen for this one — it's the scheduler, running for every connected location whether anyone's looking at the dashboard or not.

Stage 05 background — right after each new review

Each review gets sorted

Star rating against the tenant's threshold, review text against their blocklist — two lookups decide whether this one waits for a human or can go straight to a draft reply.

Stage 06 background — Azure OpenAI, westeurope

A draft reply gets written

Every classified review gets at least one candidate reply, sitting as a draft until a person decides what happens to it.

Stage 07 background — email / Teams

The owner gets pinged

Only escalated reviews page anyone — the routine auto-reply candidates just sit in the queue for whenever someone next opens it.

Stage 08 Dashboard & Review Queue screens

Someone opens the queue

The Status and Classification dropdowns on the Review Queue screen are literally this query — filter reviews, join in whatever's waiting in review_responses.

Stage 09 Review Detail screen

Approve, edit, or reject

Three buttons on the Review Detail screen, three different write patterns underneath.

Stage 10 background — after approval

The reply goes live

The last hop: InnoPeak calls Google, and the row that's been draft → pending_approval → approved gets its final state.

Stage 11 Settings screen — four tabs, four tables

The owner tunes the rules

Not a pipeline — four independent controls, each landing on its own table, none of them waiting on the others.

Stage 12 background — retention job

Old data ages out

GDPR erasure isn't a delete — it's a timestamp. The row stays for referential integrity; what it says about a person doesn't.

Every step above audit_logs

Nothing above happens quietly

Each of the other 18 tables carries an AFTER INSERT OR UPDATE OR DELETE trigger calling log_db_changes(), which writes the table name, the operation, and the old and new row as JSON into audit_logs. It's the one table in this whole journey nobody has to remember to write to — deliberately the only audit log table in this schema, rather than splitting the same accountability need across two tables where one needed manual per-endpoint annotation to ever get written to at all. The trigger also pulls tenant_id straight out of whichever row changed, so a tenant's own audit history — or a platform admin's investigation scoped to one tenant — doesn't mean scanning every row's JSON to find it.
